## Summary

Simpson's paradox — the named failure mode of pooling rates across sub-populations with unequal
exposure. A trend present in every subgroup can vanish or reverse once the subgroups are combined; it
arises when group sizes differ substantially and a lurking variable is correlated with both group
membership and outcome. The article's baseball worked example is the closest analogue to a pooled
matchup cell: Justice outhit Jeter in both 1995 and 1996 individually, yet Jeter's combined average
(.310) exceeds Justice's (.270), and the article attributes this directly to the large differences in
at-bats between the years. This is the concrete reason a superarchetype cell cannot simply be the sum
of its members' win/loss counts without a concentration diagnostic attached.

## Key passages

- Definition: "A trend appears in several groups of data but disappears or reverses when the groups
  are combined."
- Danger condition: the paradox is "particularly problematic when frequency data are unduly given
  causal interpretations", arising from unequal group sizes combined with a confounding variable.
- Worked reversal (batting averages): 1995 Justice .253 > Jeter .250; 1996 Justice .321 > Jeter .314;
  combined Jeter .310 > Justice .270 — "This phenomenon can occur when there are large differences in
  the number of at bats between the years."
